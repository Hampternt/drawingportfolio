Last Call · Interface

Phone & big screen

The six-beat round on a 390×844 phone and a 1920×1080 spectator screen. Built around three hard constraints: a hand can reach 16 cards, the phone must switch between your hand and the table in one tap, and decks are finite — counts and discards are visible at all times.

One tap between two truths

HAND and TABLE are one switch, never a scroll. The phase banner and opponent rail never move, so context survives the switch.

16 cards, no fan

A fanned hand dies past six cards. It's a grid: 3 columns up to 9 cards, 4 columns beyond that. 16 is exactly 4×4 and fits one screen with no scroll.

Edge tabs for what's on you

EVENT and QUEST live as vertical tabs on the right edge. They pulse when newly dealt or newly relevant, and slide a sheet over the content area only.

Finite decks, shown

Every deck carries a remaining count and a discard count. Under five cards it turns amber; empty reshuffles the discard and the big screen announces it.

A table, not a picture of one

The felt is one dark oval with a rim and an inner vignette — no wood, no green baize, no photograph. Seats sit on the rim in real seating order, and every card a player commits lands on the felt in front of them, angled slightly, like it was actually put down.

Order lives in the centre

Placement answers “who played what”; the centre plate answers “what happens next”. Numbered chips on the cards match the numbered rows, so nothing has to be drawn across the table. Responses carry a mint chip and sit lifted above the card they answer.

The centre is the shared state

Event stack, quest stack and the discard sit under the resolution plate as real stacked backs with counts — the three piles nobody owns, in the one place everybody already looks. Per-player deck counts stay on the right rail where they scan as a list.

Open, from designing this

Seven seats fit an oval; eight starts crowding the ends. Beyond that the table grows a second ring or the plaques drop their pull bars. Also unresolved: whether an eliminated seat stays on the felt all night or collapses so the living close ranks.

Draws are shown, not counted

Every card a player pulls flies from its deck to that player's plaque, in that deck's colour. Players can run more than one drink at a time — their plaque carries a dot per deck and pulls from each of them. Seven people drawing at once reads as a burst out of the middle of the table — you can see who drew heavy without reading a number.

Eight cards, then a number

Each plaque shows the hand as overlapping backs in the player's deck colours — up to eight fit. Past that it holds seven and adds the remainder as a figure, so a fat hand still reads as fat without the plaque growing.

Played cards land in the discard

The same flight runs in reverse when a card resolves: plaque to the dashed discard pile, which ticks up as it lands. When a deck empties, its discard flies back as a stack and the count resets.

Deck colour is not a choice

The deck you build into paints the card: a 3px top edge, the cost pip, the border tint and a tinted surface underneath the art. Pick the deck first and the card is already coloured — there is no separate colour picker to get wrong, and a card can never lie about which deck it came from.

Darken on touch, not on load

Art sits at full strength at rest with a bottom scrim carrying the name and effect. Press or hover and the whole card dims to 66% — the moment you are actually reading it is the moment the text wins. 130ms, no scale, so a 16-card grid does not wobble.

One aspect, resized on upload

Every card is 5:7. Upload anything; the crop step fits it to 640×896 and stores webp, so the phone grid, the enlarge overlay and the 1080p spectator screen all pull the same file. Under 320×448 is refused rather than upscaled.

Open, from designing this

Custom art is the obvious way to get something into a room that should not be there, so cards are per-account until every player in the room opts in — and someone still has to decide whether a reported card is removed for that room or for the account.

A, with C folded into the top

The roster is the default because a list is the only layout that does not degrade as players are added — seven rows or twelve, it scrolls, and health and incoming stay in the same column. What C got right was leading with the one thing you need before you can think at all: how much damage is about to land on you. That now sits above the list, and C stops being a separate view.

B is a toggle, not a mode

LIST / FELT sits in the roster header. The felt earns its place when an event says “the player to your left” and you have to count seats, which a list cannot answer. It caps out around six players, so above seven seats the toggle hides itself rather than shrinking the pucks past reading.

What C keeps that A drops

C sorted by relevance rather than health and wrote sentences: “Ben is on 3 — one hit finishes him.” That reads well and argues badly. Diplomacy runs on raw numbers people can point at, so A keeps the numbers and C survives only as the incoming block. Left in the doc as the road not taken.

What the detail sheet has to carry

Tapping a player has to be worth the tap, so the sheet is not a bigger row: their cards on the table, where they land in the order, deck and discard counts, and eight rounds of history — enough to argue with. Left/right arrows walk the table without going back, which is what people actually do.
