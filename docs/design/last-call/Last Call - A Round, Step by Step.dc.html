Last Call · Overview One round, three players, six beats

A round, step by step

Everything the design currently says, played out as a single worked round. Three players, round 4, mid‑game. Every number here follows the rules as written — where a number doesn't exist yet, it's marked provisional.

The table, at the start of round 4

Three decks, three different vessels, three very different positions. The bar under each name is their vessel: one segment per pull, filled means undrunk. That bar is how much they can still spend this round.

Flat 2 damage per pull, so a 3‑cost never out‑values three 1‑costs on raw numbers — it wins by being unreactable or hitting everyone. These are invented for this walkthrough; the doc has none.

Why three players is already awkward

At three, every attack is legible — there are only two people to aim at, so bluffing is thin and the react window mostly resolves into “both of them hit the low one”. Ghosts, pacts and Bar Tab all need a crowd. The walkthrough works, and it shows exactly why the doc's fallback is four minimum.

Three to five minutes end to end. Everyone is in the same beat at the same time and only lock is hidden — the clocks are backstops, and a beat ends the moment all three are ready. Turn order exists only inside resolve.

1

Draw — about 20 seconds

Discard · finish & draw, or keep

Bin whatever you don’t want, face down. Then one decision: finish what’s in front of you and open a fresh vessel — taking a full hand on top of everything you kept — or keep what you have and draw nothing. There is no other way to gain a card.

SARA keeps

Bins a dead 2‑cost, drinks nothing, draws nothing. Level 5, three cards — a 2‑cost attack, a 1‑cost attack and a 1‑cost coaster she has been saving. Comfortable, and no bomb in sight: Beer’s list is 1s and 2s only.

BEN FINISHES & DRAWS

Two pulls left and one card is a dead round, so he empties the glass and pours a fresh measure. Level 4, and five new cards on top of the 2‑cost he kept. Six cards, four pulls to spend them with — Liquor is 2s and 3s, so that is two plays at most.

MO keeps

Full cup, four cards, nothing to finish. He could down the Coke for five more cards at any moment and it would cost him nothing but the trip to the kitchen — he is choosing not to yet. Level 6, four cards.

What might happen instead

Ben keeps instead. He plays his single 2‑cost, empties the glass anyway, and starts round 5 on level 0 with nothing — a dead seat until he refills. The economy makes not drinking the losing line, which is the pressure the design wants; it is also the thing to watch hardest in playtest, because the losing player is the one being nudged to drink fastest.

2

Deal — about 10 seconds

Event public · quests private

One event flips face up for the whole table and lasts exactly this round. Next round’s event is already shown beside it, so the talking can trade on a future both parties can see. Quests are dealt privately, and not to everyone.

Why the event lands before the talking

Round of Shots is only interesting because it arrives with three minutes of negotiation still ahead of it. Deal it after the talking and it is a dice roll; deal it before, and it is the thing everyone is talking about. Last Orders being visible for next round is why Mo starts thinking about a card he will need in four minutes.

3

Diplomacy — open floor, 3 minute cap

Nothing here is binding

The app enforces none of this, which is exactly why it is worth doing. Anyone may un‑ready until the last person taps in.

BEN

“I have got nothing, I just poured.” A lie, and a cheap one — the whole table watched him finish the glass, which under the draw rule means he is holding more than anyone.

MO

Offers Sara a deal out loud: he will shield her this round if she points at Ben rather than at him. He does not mention that he is holding the only shield at the table.

SARA

Takes the deal, because she was aiming at Ben anyway — her quest needs him under 5. She reveals her 1‑cost coaster to prove she is not defenceless. That reveal is public and cannot be taken back.

What might happen instead

Nobody talks and everyone taps ready in nine seconds. That is a legitimate outcome and the beat should not fight it — but if it happens every round, the events are not doing their job and the cards are too simple to argue about.

4

Lock — up to 60 seconds

Hidden · simultaneous · then everything flips

Arm cards, tap a face on the opponent rail to aim, hit LOCK. Nobody sees anything but a tick appearing next to each name, and the moment the last player locks it all flips at once. Cards you hold back stay in hand and cost nothing to keep — the price of holding is only ever the pull you did not spend.

SARA → BEN

One 2‑cost attack at the player on 9 HP. She keeps the coaster back deliberately: she has read the fresh measure as an incoming burst.

5

Respond — 20 seconds

One layer only · loud

Only response‑flagged cards can be played, they cost pulls like anything else, and responses cannot be responded to. Twenty seconds, no stack, no priority argument. A response resolves before the thing it answers, so a countered card never happens at all.

Sara spends the coaster she showed

The 1‑cost she revealed during diplomacy: absorb 3. One more pull down. Showing it earlier cost her the surprise and bought her the deal — on balance, worth it.

Mo pays the debt he offered

The 2‑cost shield absorbs 3 more. It costs him two mouthfuls of Coke and buys a favour he can call in later. This is the Soft deck playing to win, not spectating.

What might happen instead

Mo quietly lets it through. Sara drops to 6, Ben’s glass is empty, and Mo is the only player above 15 with a full cup — the strongest position at the table, reached by breaking a promise nobody can prove he made. Whether that line is boring or brilliant is the single most useful thing a paper test could tell you.

6

Resolve — about 15 seconds

Play order · one drink instruction each

Everything left fires in turn order, top to bottom, one narrated line at a time. Then every player gets exactly one drink instruction — the total pulls they spent this round, in one line, so nobody is sipping on a timer. Cards left in hand stay there; there is nothing to bin.

What this round showed

The economy did the balancing, not a rule

Ben was the most aggressive player and is now the drunkest and the closest to out. Nothing punished him — he paid for it himself, in pulls. That's the pillar working.

The response window was the whole round

8 damage became 2 in twenty seconds. Without that beat the round is rock‑paper‑scissors; with it, it is the only twenty seconds all three players are looking at the same thing.

Burst is currently too easy to blank

Two reactions cancelled a whole fresh measure. If the Liquor player can be no‑sold this cheaply, its 3‑costs need to be unreactable — which is exactly the “wins on concentration” rule, now with a reason.

Soft needs a reason to be hittable

Mo took no damage because neither drinker had a reason to spend on him over each other. Free refills plus low threat is a strong turtle. Watch this before writing a single card.

Cards outlive the can

Ben ends the round holding the best hand at the table and unable to play any of it. That is the shape the new draw rule creates, and it is a much more interesting failure state than running out of cards.

Turn order paid Ben nothing

He acted first because he spent most, and it bought him nothing — the responses came after his cards and undid them anyway. Going first only matters if some cards can kill before a response lands. Another argument for unreactable 3‑costs.
