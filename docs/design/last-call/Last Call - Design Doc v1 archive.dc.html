Drinks · New game mode Draft 0.2 · ready to build

Last Call

A card battler for 2–8 players where your drink is your hand, the cost of a card is a mouthful of it, and the last person standing hasn't necessarily drunk the least. Everyone talks, then commits at once — and the player who spent the most acts first.

The one-line pitch

Magic-style multiplayer, but the resource you tap isn't mana — it's the can in front of you. The table negotiates out loud, everyone commits in secret, cards flip together, and a response window lets people fight back before damage lands.

Why it belongs in Drinks

Ring of Fire and 3 Man are luck engines — you obey the card. Last Call is the strategy slot: the first mode where playing well matters, and where the app is genuinely doing something a deck of cards can't.

Design pillars

Drinking is the cost, not the punishment

You never drink because you lost. You drink because you chose to do something.

Nobody waits

Eight players, one hidden commit. Everyone locks at once; only the resolution has an order.

Your drink is your deck

What's in your hand at the bar decides how you play. Beer plays nothing like liquor.

Heads up, not down

Phone = your hand, private. Reveal happens out loud, and on the TV if there is one.

1 · The vessel economy

This is the mechanic the whole game hangs off, and the one worth protecting through every later change. What's left in your vessel is what you can still do. Not a metaphor and not two systems — one number does both jobs. Drink is measured in pulls; a card costs pulls to play; playing a card drains the vessel by its cost; and an empty vessel is the only thing in the game that gives you more cards.

The whole economy, in one line
A vessel ≈ one standard drink
AN EMPTY VESSEL IS A NEW HAND

Cards cost 1, 2 or 3 pulls to play, and what's left in front of you — your level — is how much you can still spend. Eight pulls is eight cheap plays, or two bombs and a spare. You never gain a card any other way than by finishing what you're drinking and opening the next one, which is the promise the whole game rests on: the only card engine is your throat.

PULLS

The unit. Everything in the game is priced in mouthfuls of your own drink, and how many mouthfuls a vessel holds is a constant of your deck — not of the container.

COST

A card costs 1, 2 or 3 pulls to play, and playing it drains the vessel by exactly that. Your level isn't a score — it's the amount of game you have left this round.

REFILL

The only card engine. Finish what's in front of you, open a fresh vessel, take a full hand on top of what you kept. It happens at beat 1 and it is the comeback button — see 4.1.

The choice the vessel forces

Wide or heavy, out of the same can

A vessel is a fixed number of pulls and a card list spans several costs, so the interesting decision isn't which cards you're dealt — it's how you spend a can. Trickle out cheap cards over four rounds and you always have an answer; dump a bomb and a pair of 2s in one round and you can end somebody, but the can is dry and you're at the mercy of the react window.

ONE CAN, SPENT WIDE
11111111

8 plays. Answers for everything, pressure on nobody. Survives a reaction window because you can afford to lose a card to a counter.

ONE CAN, SPENT HEAVY
3221

4 plays. Enough to end somebody. One well-timed counter in the react window and a quarter of your can just evaporated.

The balance rule this creates: a 3-cost must never simply out-value three 1-costs on raw numbers, or wide play dies. It should win on concentration — things three small cards can't do, like unreactable damage, or hitting the whole table, or a hard removal — and lose on flexibility and resilience.

Worked example — the finish-and-draw swing, on a beer can (full level 8)
START OF ROUND 4

Three-eighths of a can left. Level 3, holding a 2-cost and a 1-cost. You can play both and then the can is dry. You are running out of game.

YOU FINISH & DRAW

Down the last three pulls, crack a fresh can. Level 8, and a full hand dealt on top of the two you kept. Seven cards, eight pulls to spend them with.

YOU PLAY

The 3-cost bomb and a 2-cost — five pulls. Level 3, five cards left. The biggest turn you've had all game, picked from seven cards instead of two.

END OF ROUND

Three pulls, five cards. The cards keep; the can doesn't. Next round you can spend three of those five — which is when the button comes round again.

What this buys the design
  • Self-balancing aggression. The most active player is the drunkest player, and the one holding back has the fewest options. The economy is the handicap.
  • A death spiral you can escape by drinking. Low on drink means low on plays means low on ways to earn a good turn — and finishing the vessel is the one exit. That's a genuinely interesting decision to hand a table at 1am.
  • Refilling is a play, not an interruption. Getting the next beer is timed, tactical and loud, instead of someone wandering off mid-round.
  • Cheating is social, not technical. Nobody needs the app to police sips; the table can see the can.
Consequences to hold onto
  • The app must bookkeep the level. It's no longer decoration — what you can play is derived from it, so pulls have to be tracked honestly and finishing a vessel has to be an explicit tap.
  • Cards outlive the can. A hand can grow past what you could possibly spend in one round, and that's fine — the constraint is pulls to play with, never room to hold. Sitting on a bomb with an empty vessel is the game's most common painful position.
  • Vessel size is a deck stat. Liquor's 4-pull glass isn't just less to spend, it's a much earlier refill decision. The decks differ before a single card is written.

2 · Decks

You pick your deck to match what you're actually drinking. Vessel size sets how much you can do in a round; the deck's character comes from how expensive and how violent its cards are. Exact cards are deliberately out of scope for this draft — what matters now is that the five decks feel structurally different at the table.

2.1 · The pour standard
ONE VESSEL ≈ ONE STANDARD DRINK

The decks have to be balanced against each other, and that only works if a vessel means roughly the same thing to everyone. So a vessel isn't a volume — it's a serving of alcohol, and each deck splits that serving into a different number of pulls. Liquor's glass is small because it's four pulls of something strong, not because a shot is a small drink.

PULL COUNT IS A DECK CONSTANT

A beer vessel is always 8 pulls whether you're on a 50cl or a 33cl can — the app just tells you what fraction of the tin that is. What you can spend never depends on what was in the fridge.

ABV BANDS DO THE POLICING

Each deck states the strength it's balanced for. A 9% imperial stout is not a Beer deck — that's Wine's band, and the app should say so at pick time rather than pretend.

THE APP OWNS THE MATH

You tell it the container once at seating. It never asks you to divide anything — it shows a gauge with your next pull marked on it.

Does it actually balance?

A full vessel in pure alcohol, deck by deck:

Beer 50cl @ 5% — 25ml
Cider 50cl @ 4.5% — 22ml
Wine 15cl @ 12% — 18ml
Liquor 4cl @ 40% — 16ml

Within about 1.5× end to end, and the heavier vessels are the ones with more pulls to spend it over. Close enough that the handicap multiplier can cover the rest.

Edge cases worth stating out loud
  • Cocktails and jugs. A drink built on more than one measure isn't a big vessel — it's several. A four-measure jug is four Liquor vessels, and refilling means finishing one glass of it.
  • Mixing decks mid-game. Allowed, at a round boundary only: finish your vessel, then switch. Switching is a free refill, which is exactly the right price.
  • Low-and-no. Anything under 0.5% is Soft regardless of what the label says it's imitating.

Soft is not a pity deck. It's the designated driver, the one on antibiotics, the one who's had enough — still fully in the game, playing a genuinely strong support role. It should be picked sometimes by people who could drink. That's the test of whether it's designed right.

Decks also differ by their cost spread. Beer's list is 1s and 2s only — the attrition deck literally has no bomb in it. Liquor is 2s and 3s, so its 4-pull glass is two plays and a decision. Cider and Soft run 1s and 2s with a single 3; Wine spreads all three. Cost spread is the cheapest, strongest lever for making a deck feel different before a single card is written. Decks are finite and per-player: each carries a remaining count and a discard pile, the count turns amber under five, and an empty deck reshuffles its discards.

3 · Life, damage, and how a game ends

20 HP

Everyone starts equal regardless of deck. Big enough for comebacks, small enough that a 5-player game lands in 20–30 minutes.

0 = out

You're eliminated, you finish what's left in your vessel, and you become a ghost — see below. Last player standing wins the game.

Ghosts

Eliminated players get one vote per round to add +1 damage to any attack already in flight. Keeps the first person out at the table instead of on Instagram.

The tension to keep an eye on

Winning costs you drink and losing gets you out early — so a passive turtle strategy is mathematically sober and socially awful. Two levers if playtesting shows turtling: an escalating Last Call clock (from round 8, everyone takes 1 damage per round, so passivity kills you), or a hold penalty (unspent pulls at refill cost HP). Prefer the clock — it's one rule and it creates an ending.

4 · The round — draw, deal, talk, lock, respond, resolve

Six beats. The first three are setup and negotiation, the last three are the fight. Only lock is hidden — everything before it is out loud, and that is the point. Beats advance the moment everyone is ready; the clock is a backstop against the person rolling a cigarette, not a pace-setter.

This is a deliberately slower round than the earlier draft. Diplomacy costs time, so a round now runs 3–5 minutes instead of 90 seconds. The trade is intentional: fewer rounds per game, bigger cards, and every play carrying enough weight to be worth arguing about beforehand.

BEAT 1
Draw

Discard what you don't want. Finish your vessel to draw a fresh hand on top of what you kept.

BEAT 2
Deal

The round's event flips face up for the table. Quests are dealt privately — to some players, not all.

BEAT 3
Diplomacy

Talk. Deal, threaten, lie, show a card if you want to. No clock until someone calls it.

BEAT 4
Lock

Cards and targets, in secret. Everything flips at once when the last player locks.

BEAT 5
Respond

Blocks, counters, traps. Responses always resolve before anything they answer.

BEAT 6
Resolve

Remaining effects fire in play order. Damage lands, quests check, drink instructions go out.

4.1 · Draw — and the price of a new hand
FINISH THE VESSEL, KEEP THE CARDS, DRAW AGAIN

Drawing is not automatic and it is not free. In the draw beat you may discard anything you don't want, and then you have one decision: empty what's in front of you and draw a fresh hand, on top of everything you kept. That is the only way to grow a hand. Don't finish it, and you play the round with what you already have.

DISCARD

Bin as much as you like, face down, before anyone sees it. Dead cards aren't a punishment — they're the cost of having drawn wide last time.

FINISH & DRAW

Empty the vessel, crack a new one, take a full hand in addition to your kept cards. The comeback button and the only card engine in the game.

HOLD

Drink nothing, draw nothing. Perfectly legal, gets quieter every round, and is what the event deck exists to punish.

Opening mulligan — first round only

At the start of the game you may throw your whole hand back and redraw, as many times as you like, until you're happy with it — no cost, no penalty, no drink. Nobody should lose a night's game to an opening hand. The table waits; it takes seconds and it happens once.

4.2 · Deal

Events are public, quests are not

  • Events are table-wide and last exactly one round. A new one is dealt every round, replacing the last — never two at once. Dealt before diplomacy, so it's the thing everyone is negotiating around.
  • Quests are private, and not everyone gets one. An uneven deal is the feature: the table can never assume what anyone is playing for. You keep a quest until you complete it or discard it, then draw a replacement at the next deal.
  • Some events are table-wide quests — a public objective every player can chase for one round, which turns the whole diplomacy beat into a race or a cartel.
4.3 · Diplomacy — the new heart of the round

The part that isn't in anyone's hand

Open floor. Form alliances, promise not to attack, name a target, threaten, show a card to prove you're not bluffing, or bluff about one you don't hold. Nothing said here is binding — the app enforces none of it, which is exactly why it's worth doing.

  • Ends when every player taps Ready. Anyone may un-ready until the last person taps in.
  • Revealing a card is a real action — it's shown on the big screen, so you can't un-show it.
  • A soft 3-minute cap exists only so a stalled table gets pushed to lock.
4.4 · Lock — and the order everything happens in

Most cards played goes first

Everyone commits in secret, everything flips at once — but effects no longer resolve simultaneously. Turn order for the round is set by how many cards you played, most first. Commit hard and you act first; hold back and you act late but informed. Ties are broken at random, and the roll is shown. Priority may not matter most rounds, but when it does the rule is already written down and nobody is arguing at 1am.

1st
Ben — 3 cards

Emptied his glass to do it. Acts before anyone can spend on a better answer.

2nd
Sara — 2 cards

Her second card lands into a board Ben has already changed.

3rd
Mo — 2 cards

Tied with Sara, lost the roll. The app shows the die so it reads as luck, not a bug.

4th
Kit — 0 cards

Played nothing, so acts last and does nothing. Passing has an order too.

The tension this creates: going first is a reward for spending, and spending is drinking. The player acting first is reliably the player who has drunk the most this round — the same self-balancing pressure as the economy, now in the turn order too.

4.5 · Respond — before anything resolves

Once every play is face up and the order is known, anyone holding a response card may play it: a block, a counter, a redirect, a trap that was waiting for exactly this. Responses take priority and resolve before the thing they answer — a countered card never happens at all.

  • One layer only. Responses cannot be responded to. No stack, no priority argument, no rules lawyer.
  • Response cards are ordinary cards flagged response-only — you must already be holding one, which is what makes the draw beat a real gamble.
  • You may respond on someone else's behalf. Shielding a stranger is a diplomacy move with a receipt.
4.6 · Resolve — rules that keep it sane
  • Everything left fires in play order, top to bottom. The app narrates one line per effect so the table can follow it.
  • Targets are locked at reveal. If your target dies before your card resolves, it fizzles — a real risk of acting late.
  • Quests check at end of resolve. Completing one is announced; what it was is not.
  • One drink instruction per player at the end of the round, never mid-beat. Drinking is never blocking.
  • Simultaneous kills mean a shared win.
What changed from the first draft

This six-beat round supersedes the 90-second five-beat draft, and section 1 has been restated to match it. Gone: the per-round pull budget, the cost racks and the draw allocator, the separate chug action, and the end-of-round spill. In their place: one draw rule — finish the vessel or don't — and a level that gates what you can play rather than what you can hold. Still open: at 3–5 minutes a round, 20 HP may be too much game. Expect 5–8 rounds, which probably means bigger cards and 12–15 HP.

5 · Handicaps

A per-player cost multiplier, set at seating and visible to everyone. It reads as a competitive handicap rather than a health warning, which is the only way it'll actually get used.

×0.5
LIGHTWEIGHT

Cards cost half the pulls, rounded up. Same hand, same damage.

×1
STANDARD

Printed cost. The default for everyone.

×2
HEAVYWEIGHT

Double cost. Self-imposed, or the table's punishment for last game's winner.

×0
SOFT DECK

Not a multiplier — a deck. The honest way to play sober.

Deliberately not doing: forced water rounds, slow-down nudges, cutoff warnings. The vessel drain bar already tells the truth about how much someone has drunk, and that's a more useful signal than a lecture.

6 · The party layer

Everything so far is a good card game that happens to involve drinking. What makes it a party game is the stuff that isn't in anyone's hand: rules that change under the table's feet, and reasons to lie to each other. Two systems, deliberately different in visibility — one is shouted at everyone, the other is whispered to one person.

Public · loud · random
EVENTS

A one-round modifier the app deals to the whole table at beat 2, before diplomacy. Everyone sees it, everyone negotiates around it. Chaos, but shared chaos — nobody feels singled out. Called house rules in earlier drafts.

Private · quiet · chosen
QUESTS

A secret objective only you can see, kept until you complete it or bin it. Not everyone gets one — the uneven deal is what stops the table reading anyone's motives. This is where the lying lives. Called tabs in earlier drafts.

6.1 · Events — the round modifier

One event is dealt face up at beat 2, before diplomacy, and it lasts exactly one round — the next deal replaces it. Never two at once. Landing it before the talking is the whole point: it's the thing the table negotiates around, not a thing that happens to you. The good ones don't add damage — they bend the economy or the information, because that's what makes people talk. Some are table-wide quests, which turn the diplomacy beat into a race or a cartel.

6.2 · Tabs — the secret half

One tab is dealt privately at seating; you draw a new one whenever you settle the old one. They pay in HP and pulls — never in winning — so the win condition stays “last one standing” and a tab is a way to get there, not a second game. Settling a tab is announced publicly (“Sara settled a tab”) but never explains what it was, which is the whole trick: the table knows you're up to something and has to guess what.

Vendettas

Aimed at one person, and they don't know.

  • Land the killing hit on Ben. +4 HP
  • Ben ends a round on 5 HP or less. +2 HP
  • Never damage Ben all game — and outlive him. +4 HP
Habits

A style you have to play in, secretly.

  • Refill twice before round 6. +3 HP
  • Win three rounds without playing a card. +4 pulls
  • Hold a 3-cost for four straight rounds, then play it. +3 HP
Favours

Someone else has to do well. You can't tell them why.

  • Sara finishes above you. +4 HP now, cash on her exit
  • Nobody at the table hits 0 for three rounds. +2 HP
  • Buy the Soft player a real drink. Table decides

Unsettled tabs are revealed at the end of the game — the recap is much better when everyone finds out that Ben spent nine rounds trying not to hurt them.

6.3 · The one worth building first

Pacts — a secret alliance you have to find

At seating, the app quietly pairs two players and deals them matching pact tabs: “If you and your partner are the last two standing, you both win.” Neither is told who the other is. You have to work it out from play — who keeps not attacking you, who shields you at a cost that made no sense — and signal back without the table catching on. Get it wrong and you've spent the game protecting an enemy.

WHY IT WORKS HERE

Simultaneous commit already hides intent. A pact costs nothing to implement — it's two tabs and a shared win check.

THE SIGNAL PROBLEM

Give each pact one free wink: once per game you may send your partner a one-word ping. If you guess wrong, they get it and now they know.

THE SAFETY VALVE

Only ever one pact per game, and only at 5+ players. Two pacts in a six-player game is four people not playing the game.

Rollout: events first — they're pure server logic, need no UI beyond a banner, and they're testable on paper tonight. Quests need a private card on the phone and a completion check per round. Pacts land last, and only if the group is the kind that enjoys being lied to. All three ship off by default, toggled at room setup, because a first game should be legible.

7 · Screens

Last Call slots into the existing three-tab room shell rather than replacing it. The GAME tab becomes the board, ROOM relabels to TABLE while a game runs (same pattern 3 Man already uses), and STANDINGS is untouched. The big screen is an enhancement, never a requirement — every piece of information on the TV also exists on the phone, just smaller.

Phone — the GAME tab, top to bottom
  1. Phase banner — beat name, round counter, and a six-segment bar showing where in the round you are. The one element that must be readable at arm's length.
  2. Opponent rail — row of up to 7 opponents: name, HP, a vessel strip in their deck colour, card count, and a lock tick during commit. Tap one to target.
  3. Content area — changes per beat. At reveal it's every played card with an arrow to its target. This is the screenshot moment.
  4. Your vessel — an amber card sitting directly above the action bar, one segment per pull, with the count in mono and one line explaining what you can do about it. The decision and its price in the same glance.
  5. Your hand — a grid, never a fan: three columns up to nine cards, four beyond, sixteen the practical ceiling. Cost chip and type tag on each. Tap to arm, tap an opponent to aim, LOCK to commit.
  6. Action bar — the beat's primary actions, then a HAND / TABLE segmented switch in the thumb zone. At beat 1 the two primaries share a row and the drinking one is amber: FINISH & DRAW against KEEP.
Big screen — the overview phones can't give
  • The arena. All players as HP columns around the edge, attacks drawn as arcs across the middle at reveal.
  • The clock. Huge phase countdown — the thing that stops eight people asking “are we waiting for someone?”
  • Lock lights. One dot per player during commit, so the table can see who's holding it up and shout at them.
  • Kill feed. Short line per resolution: “Sara → Ben, 4”. Everything else is on your phone.

Reuses /room/{code}/screen, the QR join box and the same SSE stream. No second connection, no second auth.

8 · Between nights

Rivalries

Head-to-head kill records between any two players, surfaced as a line on the pre-game seating screen: “Ben has finished you 6 times. You've got him 2.” Cheap to compute from the play log, disproportionately fun.

Titles

Earned nicknames shown under your name in the rail — awarded for patterns, not milestones. The Kingmaker (most assists on someone else's kill), Featherweight (won on the fewest pulls spent), Bottomless (most refills in a night).

Night recap

One shareable card generated when the room ends: winner, total pulls by the table, the biggest single hit, the longest rivalry swing. Read it hungover, send it to the group chat.

Lifetime counters

Pulls fold into the existing drink/shot totals on the landing page: 1 vessel emptied = 1 drink, so Last Call nights count alongside Ring of Fire nights instead of forking the stat.

10 · Open questions

The things worth settling before any of this is worth building. Answers change the UI, so they come before the mockups.

01
Does a hand have a ceiling?
Nothing caps hand size now that spill is gone, so a player who refills three rounds running could be sitting on fifteen cards they can only dribble out three pulls at a time. The screens assume sixteen is the practical ceiling. Recommendation: no hard cap for v1 — the pull cost of playing is the real limiter — but watch for the hoarder in playtesting.
02
Are decks fixed lists, or do you build one before the game?
Deckbuilding is the most Magic-like thing available and also the fastest way to make a party game unplayable at a party. Recommendation: fixed for v1.
03
Can two people share a deck?
Five people drinking beer is the normal case, so yes — but it means decks are per-player instances, not shared piles. Simplifies shuffling; loses the “cards run out” drama.
04
How long is a game actually meant to be?
20 HP at 3–5 minutes a round is well over half an hour, which is a long time to hold a table. Recommendation: drop to 12–15 HP and make cards hit harder, so a game is 5–8 rounds. Settle this at the paper playtest.
05
What happens when someone's phone dies mid-game?
Auto-pass on timeout is the cheap answer, but a player who's passing forever is a dead seat. Probably: two missed commits and you're folded out, HP intact.
06
Is the name right?
“Last Call” is a placeholder that happens to describe both the bar and the win condition. Alternatives worth a moment: Pour, Rounds, Bottoms Up.
07
Do tabs pay in HP, or in the night standings?
HP keeps it inside the match and makes a quest immediately worth chasing. Standings points make tabs a reason to keep playing rounds you're losing — but split the game's attention in two. Recommendation: HP for v1, standings later if a night ever needs a meta.
08
Should the table see the next event coming?
An event every round is a lot of chaos to absorb cold. Showing next round's event alongside this one makes diplomacy far richer — you can strike deals about a future you can both see. Costs nothing to implement. Recommendation: deal one, telegraph the next.

11 · Still undefined

Section 10 lists the questions worth arguing about. This is the flatter list — the things the design has no answer for at all. Each carries a fallback, the answer we default to if nobody decides, so none of them can block a playtest.

Cards

No card exists yet. Every deck needs a real list: name, cost in pulls, damage or effect, whether it is playable in the react window, and how many copies sit at each cost.

Fallback if nobody decides: Beer only, ~12 cards, on index cards. Nothing else can be balanced until this exists.

Damage numbers

What does a 1-cost actually hit for against 20 HP? A 3-cost? The rule that a 3-cost must not out-value three 1-costs is currently a principle with no numbers under it.

Fallback if nobody decides: Pick a baseline — 1-cost = 1 damage — and derive the rest from the paper test.

Targeting rules

Can several people hit the same target in one round? Can you target yourself? Does an attack split, or does each card pick exactly one player? Table-wide cards need their own rule.

Fallback if nobody decides: One card, one target; stacking allowed; self-target only for heals and shields.

The react window

Which cards are reactions, and how many a deck should carry so that holding one is a real gamble rather than a certainty. Whether you can react on someone else's behalf is written down but untested.

Fallback if nobody decides: Reactions are normal cards flagged react-only; you must already be holding one.

Deck size

Cost spread is called the strongest lever in the doc, but no deck has a stated card list, a count of copies per cost, or a size for a fresh draw. The screens show five cards; nothing justifies five.

Fallback if nobody decides: 24 cards a deck, draw five, discards reshuffle when it runs dry.

Ghost powers

One vote per round for +1 damage is written down. Not written down: whether ghosts see hidden hands, whether tabs can still name them, whether they count for a pact win.

Fallback if nobody decides: Ghosts see everything and count for nothing.

Handicap fairness

A ×0.5 player gets the same hand for half the drink, and nothing currently stops everyone from setting themselves to lightweight.

Fallback if nobody decides: Handicaps are set by the table at seating, not by the player.

Tab supply

How many tabs exist per category, how repeats are avoided across a night, and what happens to a tab whose named target is eliminated before it settles.

Vessel honesty

The app tracks pulls but nothing verifies one happened. Whether a skipped pull is a rules problem or a social one should be a decision on the record, not an accident.

Fallback if nobody decides: Social. The table can see the can — never build enforcement.

Table minimum

The doc says 2–8, but the react window, ghosts and pacts all assume a crowd. Whether 2–3 players is supported at all is undefined.

Fallback if nobody decides: Four minimum for a real game; 2–3 is a stripped duel mode, or nothing.

Safety line

Finishing a vessel to draw is a mechanic that rewards drinking fast. Whatever the stance is — age gate, opening screen, a per-night pull ceiling — it needs to be stated.

Fallback if nobody decides: Decide it before anyone outside the group plays it.

Look of a card

The screens fix the card's anatomy — cost chip, type tag, name, one line of rules text, 5:7 art slot — but no card has been written to it and no art direction exists. Whether art earns the upload flow at all is unproven.

Fallback if nobody decides: Text-only cards in the existing app style for the first build.
